The law, in one line

In 1952 John von Neumann wrote down a result so clean it looks like a mistake. For any two-dimensional cellular structure whose walls move under their own curvature — soap film, metal grains, magnetic domains — the rate at which a cell gains or loses area depends on nothing but its number of sides:

dA/dt = k · (n − 6)

Not its area. Not how round or ragged it is. Not who its neighbours are. A hundred-sided monster and a speck both obey the same arithmetic. Six sides is the fixed point, because six is the only polygon whose 120° corners meet a soap film's equilibrium angle without any wall having to bend. Everything else is curved, and curvature is appetite.

So a five-sided bubble is not unlucky. It is doomed on arrival, and the clock started before you poured the glass.

Where the simulation frayed, and one relation that did not fit

Triangles fall short. Three-sided cells shrank at 39% of the rate the relation demands, and four-sided at 81%. This is not a refutation of von Neumann; it is the lattice running out of room. By the time a cell is down to three sides it occupies a handful of pixels, and a wall one pixel thick cannot curve. The relation describes a continuum, and the simulation stops being one at exactly the moment a cell is about to die. Including that bin drags R2 from 0.9996 down to 0.956, which is why the headline fit is quoted over n = 5…9 and the shortfall is quoted here rather than buried.

Lewis's law did not fit this system. In 1928 Frederic Lewis reported that in cucumber epidermis and similar tissues a cell's area rises roughly linearly with its side count, A(n) ∝ n − n₀. Fitted against a quadratic on this simulation's data, the quadratic won in all three seeds: R2 = 0.977 linear against 0.992 quadratic.

That is a statement about this simulation and nothing more. Lewis's law is an empirical regularity measured in particular biological tissues; a Potts foam on a lattice is not a cucumber, and the literature has long treated the relation as approximate and system-dependent rather than exact. The honest reading is that this is not a system Lewis's straight line describes well — not that Lewis was wrong.

Aboav–Weaire held, emphatically. The claim that a cell's neighbours are systematically opposite to it — that big-n cells are ringed by small-n cells — predicts that n·m(n) is linear in n. Measured: R2 ≥ 0.9995 with a ≈ 1.16. Seven-sided cells really are surrounded by five-sided ones. Prosperity in this world is built directly on top of your neighbours' dying.

Why the mean sits at six, and what makes it leak

The most suspicious number on this page is the one that barely moves. Cells are born, starve, and vanish by the thousand, and ⟨n⟩ hovers at 6.000 throughout. That is not the simulation equilibrating — it is arithmetic that has almost no choice.

Where three walls meet at a point (and in a foam they always do — four-way junctions are unstable and snap apart within a frame), Euler's formula for a surface with no boundary forces V − E + F = 0, and three walls per vertex forces 2E = 3V. Substitute, and the average face gets exactly six edges. On a torus that is not an approximation; it is an identity, and any vertex with more than three walls can only push the mean down.

This page originally claimed the mean never rises above six. That was wrong, and the test suite is what caught it. At 512 sites the claim held across all 69 samples. Pushed harder — a 256-site lattice, three seeds, 168 samples — the mean does creep above six, in 4 samples, by at most 0.018.

The cause is measurable rather than mysterious. Two cells that meet at a single lattice site are recorded as neighbours, even though topologically they meet at a three-way vertex and share no edge at all. Require two sites of shared wall instead of one and every excursion disappears: 0 of 168 samples above six, against 4 of 168 when one site is enough. The ceiling is real. What leaks is the lattice's rendering of it. There is now a test asserting exactly that, so if the effect ever changes, this paragraph fails with it.

Which sharpens the whole thing into something bleaker than it first looks. The average cell sits at break-even, forever, held there by counting rather than by physics. Every cell that climbs to seven sides does it by pushing someone else to five. The system is not growing. It is only redistributing, and it pays for each survivor with a death.

How much this page is actually worth

Nothing here overturns anything. Von Neumann's relation is a theorem about curvature-driven cellular networks in the continuum, and it is not in question. This page did not test it. It tested whether a lattice Monte Carlo simulation reproduces it — a different and much smaller question — and the answer was yes, to four significant figures, over the range where the lattice is fine enough to resolve a curved wall.

So when something here disagrees with a published result, the ordering of suspects is not close. First the simulation, then the measurement, then my arithmetic, and only then the century of work that came before. Every disagreement on this page resolved in one of the first three: the triangles are a discretisation artifact, the excursions above six are the lattice recording an edge where there is only a vertex, and Lewis's line not fitting a Potts foam says something about Potts foams.

Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. A page rebuilt in a day, on one machine, by one model, is not extraordinary evidence — it is a demonstration. Read every number below as "this is what this code did", not "this is how the world is". The tests can check the first with real rigour. They cannot check the second, and neither can I.

Where you have already seen this

The head on your beer collapses on this schedule. Small bubbles are five-sided, five-sided bubbles shrink, and their disappearance hands sides to the survivors, which is why a fine dense foam coarsens into a few fat cells before it goes flat.

The steel in a wrench does the same thing at 900 °C, only slower and in a solid. Metallurgists call it grain growth, and it is the reason annealing has to be timed: strength scales roughly as 1/√grain-size (Hall–Petch), grain size grows as √t, so leaving the metal in the furnace too long makes it measurably weaker. The micrograph of an over-annealed alloy and a photograph of stale beer foam are, statistically, the same picture.

Also on the list: mud cracks and columnar basalt, where the Giant's Causeway settles near six sides for the same reason; epithelial tissue, where the hexagon-dominated packing of cells in a fruit-fly wing follows the same census; magnetic domains in a recording medium; and the bubble rafts Bragg used in the 1940s as a physical analogue computer for dislocations in crystals — which turns out to have been a better analogy than he could have known.

Method, and what this does not show

The engine is a Q-state Potts model with a conserved cell identity per grain, seeded by a breadth-first Voronoi partition. Each sweep rebuilds the list of boundary sites and offers each one a flip to a randomly chosen neighbour's state, accepted if it does not raise the interface energy and otherwise with probability e−ΔE/kT. Time is measured in boundary sweeps, area in lattice sites, so k carries those units and is not comparable to a physical γM.

Topology is extracted from four-connected adjacency. Two cells count as neighbours in the histogram once they share at least three lattice sites of wall, which suppresses corner contacts; the panel's ⟨n⟩ counts every contact instead, because that is the quantity Euler constrains. Statistics start after a warmup to clear the artificial Voronoi initial condition, during which four-fold vertices are still abundant and ⟨n⟩ runs as low as 5.84.

What this does not show: real foams drain and rupture under gravity and real metals have anisotropic boundary energies and pinning particles, none of which is modelled here. The claim being tested is narrower than "beer behaves like steel" — it is that both are curvature-driven cellular networks, and that in such a network the side count is the whole story. That part measured true to four significant figures.

No external libraries, no network calls, no build step. The simulation lives in grain-model.js and knows nothing about canvases; the drawing lives in renderer.js. That separation is what makes the tests below possible.
