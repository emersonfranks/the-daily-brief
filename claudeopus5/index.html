The attack with no attacker

a social network running out of attention ↔ a network under a targeted strike on its hubs

Take a network and delete its most connected nodes one by one and it shatters. That is a well-known act of sabotage. Now take the same network, delete nobody, attack nobody, and simply let the busiest people run out of the attention needed to keep their ties alive. Below a certain capacity the second picture stops being distinguishable from the first.

The honest version, which is what this page actually measured: that only happens below a threshold. Above it, attention scarcity does no more damage than the same number of people dropping out at random. The switch between the two is abrupt, and finding that is what killed this page's first draft.

The experiment

Below are three copies of the same 300-person network, drawn in the same positions so you can compare them directly. One dot is one person. Bigger dots have more ties. A line is a tie between two people. Bright blue means "still joined to the single largest connected piece of the network"; dim slate means "connected to somebody, but cut off from the main body"; a red cross means the person has been deleted outright.

The thesis, in plain English

A network of people is held together by its hubs — the handful of individuals who know everybody. Take those hubs away and the rest of the network falls into unconnected fragments. It has been known since 2000 that this is exactly why networks like the internet are simultaneously almost immune to random breakdowns and desperately fragile to deliberate ones.

The surprise is that you do not need the deliberate one. Hubs are hubs because they have many ties, and having many ties is precisely what makes each individual tie unaffordable when attention is short. So scarcity finds the hubs on its own. It targets them for the same reason an attacker would — because they are big — without anybody intending anything.

But scarcity is not always an attack, and this is where the page's first draft was wrong. When there is attention to spare, hubs going quiet costs the network almost nothing: the ties that die are mostly ones nothing was depending on, and the damage is indistinguishable from the same number of people dropping out at random. Below a critical capacity that stops being true, sharply. The same mechanism, applied a little harder, changes from harmless to catastrophic without passing through anything in between.

Is this analogy mathematical or empirical? Mathematical, and only half-measured. Both systems here are percolation on a graph, and what is being compared is one simulation against two simulated controls on the same graph — not two independent bodies of field data. The attention side is a deliberately simplified reduction of a mechanism from a 2026 preprint, not a reimplementation of that paper's model, and the numbers on this page are properties of that reduction. What is empirical is the ingredient: people really do have a finite capacity for keeping ties active, measured across nineteen months of communication records from about twenty million people. What is not established here is that any real social network has ever been observed crossing this threshold.

Underneath

How one person going quiet is modelled

Each person splits their attention evenly over the ties they have, so someone with k ties gives each one a share of 1 / (k + a). A shared activity happens on a tie only if that share clears a threshold and the partner looks likely enough to turn up — the share is discounted by the fraction of the partner's own ties that are still active. The slider sets the threshold as 1 / (capacity + a), which means that in the very first round a tie needs both of its people to have at most capacity ties.

Then it cascades. When ties die, the people at their ends look less reliable, which kills further ties, and so on until nothing more changes. That fixed point is what the first panel draws. Nobody is ever removed from the network; people are only ever left with no live tie. a = 1 throughout.

How far past the first round the cascade reaches is a measurement, not an input, and it turns out to reach a very long way — see the failure below.

The named science this rests on

Percolation on a degree sequence. Michael Molloy and Bruce Reed showed in 1995 that a random graph with a given degree sequence has a giant connected component precisely when κ = ⟨k²⟩ / ⟨k⟩ exceeds 2, and loses it when κ falls to 2. Everything on this page is that one number moving.

Robust yet fragile. Réka Albert, Hawoong Jeong and Albert-László Barabási established in 2000 that scale-free networks survive enormous rates of random failure and collapse under the removal of a small number of their best-connected nodes; Reuven Cohen, Keren Erez, Daniel ben-Avraham and Shlomo Havlin worked out the critical attack fraction analytically the following year. Those two results are the "attacker" and "bad luck" panels.

Attention is finite, and this was measured. Giovanna Miritello, Rubén Lara, Manuel Cebrián and Esteban Moro found a finite communication capacity per person in nineteen months of records covering roughly twenty million people, cutting against the assumption that connectivity simply grows.

The recent anchor. Alexei Vazquez's August 2026 preprint is where the pairing comes from. Making participation strategic gives a queueing model of human activity a discontinuous absorbing transition, and on a network the attention split 1/(k + a) fixes a critical degree beyond which no coupled state exists — so the solitary phase percolates by the Molloy–Reed criterion with the second moment truncated at that degree. The paper's own description of this is the sentence this page is named after: formally an attack on hubs, with no attacker.

What failed, with the numbers

The first thesis died. The draft claim was that attention scarcity would look like a hub attack throughout. It does not. At a capacity of — the no-attacker network keeps — of itself connected, bad luck keeps —, and the targeted attack keeps only —. That puts the attack-likeness at — — sitting on the random control, not the attack. Drop the capacity by just — more steps, though, and at capacity — the same three numbers become — with no attacker, — under attack and — under bad luck — an attack-likeness of —. The claim had to become a claim about a threshold. It is checked below as plenty-looks-like-bad-luck, so it cannot quietly revert.

This simulation does not reproduce the preprint's truncation. The source describes the coupled network as the original one with its degree sequence truncated at a critical degree. If that were the whole story, deleting exactly the over-capacity people outright would do the same damage as letting them fall silent. It does not come close. At that same crossover capacity of —, only — people (— of the network) are over their limit; deleting exactly those people leaves — of the network connected, while letting them go quiet leaves —. Across five graph seeds the gap is never less than 60 percentage points.

Whose fault is that? Mine, and in this order. First the model: the reliability discount here is a synchronous cascade on one finite 300-node graph, and the preprint's percolation statement is a mean-field description of a coupled phase, which is not the same object. Second the reduction: this page implements a consequence of the paper's rule, not the paper's queueing dynamics, so it has no right to reproduce the paper's algebra. Only after those comes any question about the source. The named artifact is that a distrust cascade strips ties from people who are nowhere near their own limit, and pure degree truncation cannot do that.

What is not claimed. Not that real friendship networks collapse this way; that is untested here. Not that the paper is wrong. Not that the threshold sits at any particular number of friends — the capacity axis on this page is a parameter of a toy, not a count of human relationships.

What would have changed my mind

Written down before the simulation was run, and left standing whether or not it survived:

  1. If the first people to fall silent had not been the high-degree ones, the whole pairing would have been dead on arrival. Survived: they are six to eight times the mean degree.
  2. If the no-attacker network had tracked the targeted attack across the whole capacity range. Failed. It tracks the random control instead, until it abruptly does not.
  3. If random dropout had shattered the network too, the page would have measured nothing but generic dilution. Survived: the random control stays far above the attack curve throughout.
  4. If the collapse had not coincided with κ crossing 2, this would be some other phenomenon wearing percolation as a costume. Survived, on all five seeds.

Every one of those is encoded as a runnable check below, including the one that failed.
