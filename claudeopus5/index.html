The shortcut that slows you down

A city adds a fast new road and every commute gets longer. An engineer cuts a load-bearing string and the hanging weight rises. These are the same event. Both systems slide downhill into the minimum of a potential function, and in neither case is that potential the thing anybody wanted.

The experiment

Below are two machines that have nothing to do with each other: a commuter road network on the left, a weight hanging from springs on the right. Press the button. It deletes one connection from each — the free shortcut in the road, the string joining the two springs — and you should expect both to get worse. Watch the commute in minutes and the depth in centimetres. Both improve.

Shortcut open, string intact

What is actually going on

Neither of these systems is trying to give you what you want. Each one is falling into the bottom of a bowl, and the bowl is shaped by something other than your interests.

The drivers are not minimising the average commute. Each one is minimising their own commute, and Robert Rosenthal showed in 1973 that a crowd of selfish route-choosers is mathematically identical to a ball rolling downhill in a function — his potential, the sum over every road of its travel time added up one car at a time. Every driver who switches to a faster route lowers that potential by exactly what they personally saved. The crowd stops when nobody can go downhill any further. That resting point is a Nash equilibrium, and nothing anywhere in the process ever consulted the average commute.

The rig is not minimising how high the weight hangs. It is minimising elastic energy plus gravitational energy, and force balance is just that minimisation happening in real time. When the red string is intact, the two springs are in series: each one carries the whole 10 N load, each stretches the full amount, and the sags add up. Cut it and the load splits down two paths — the springs end up in parallel, each carrying 5 N, each stretching half as far. Fewer newtons per spring, less stretch, higher weight.

Take a connection away and a potential minimiser is not obliged to get worse. That is the whole trick, and it is why the two pictures above respond to one button.

Is this an analogy or the same mathematics? The same mathematics. Dietrich Braess described the traffic version in 1968; Joel Cohen and Paul Horowitz published the spring-and-string version in Nature in 1991, showing the mechanical and electrical networks reproduce it exactly. It is not that these two systems are vaguely alike — they are the same constrained-minimisation problem wearing different units. What is not established here is anything empirical about real cities. Road closures in Seoul and New York are widely cited as real instances, but this page contains no traffic data and tests no such claim.

Where the paradox lives, and where it does not

The story is usually told as though a free road always hurts. It does not. Sweeping demand from empty to gridlocked, the penalty exists only between 3,000 and 9,000 drivers, peaking at 22.5 min at 4,500 drivers. Below that the shortcut is a real improvement. Above it, the shortcut is so congested that the equilibrium abandons it.

Shaded band: the demand range in which opening the shortcut makes things worse.

Underneath

The numbers, worked by hand, and why the simulation is allowed to disagree slightly

With the shortcut shut, demand splits evenly, so each of the 4,000 drivers meets 2,000 cars on their jamming road: 2000/100 = 20 minutes, plus the flat 45, giving 65 minutes.

Open the shortcut and the route HOME→A→B→WORK uses both jamming roads and nothing else. If everyone takes it, each jamming road carries all 4,000 cars: 40 + 40 = 80 minutes. Nobody can escape — leaving for the old route costs 40 + 45 = 85. So 80 minutes is a genuine equilibrium, and it is fifteen minutes worse than the road network without the new road in it.

The three regimes fall out of the same algebra. Everyone funnels through the shortcut while demand is under 4,500. Between 4,500 and 9,000 the equilibrium is mixed and pinned at exactly twice the fixed cost, 90 minutes. Past 9,000 the shortcut is abandoned entirely. The simulation solves this by best-response dynamics instead, moving one driver at a time, and it lands within 0.02 min of the closed form — the gap is entirely because drivers come in whole numbers.

A prediction of mine that failed, and what it cost me

Before measuring, I wrote down that cutting the string would leave the rig at a higher total potential energy — my reasoning being that a system forced into a stiffer arrangement should sit higher in its own landscape. It is wrong. Measured, energy goes from −620.13 to −760.06: cutting leaves the rig lower. The springs shed far more elastic energy than gravity gives back from the shorter fall.

The correction matters more than the mistake. You cannot compare the two potentials at all, because cutting the string does not move the system within one landscape — it replaces the landscape. The same is true on the road: the potential of the shut network and the potential of the open one are numbers about different games. Each system faithfully descends its own potential, and that is exactly why neither potential can tell you which world you would rather live in. This failure is kept as a live test, no-common-currency, which will start failing the moment the claim stops being true.

What would have proved this page wrong

The falsifiers were written down before anything was measured:

  • If opening the shortcut had left the equilibrium commute unchanged or shorter at 4,000 drivers, the thesis was dead.
  • If a single driver's improving switch had ever raised the Rosenthal potential, the claim that selfish routing is a descent process was dead. Across 810 recorded steps at eight demand levels, none did.
  • If cutting the string had lowered the weight at the shipped geometry, the mechanical half was dead.
  • If the penalty had persisted at every demand, the honest framing would have been the reverse of what is written above.

One threshold in the code was set by measurement rather than taste: the rig solver runs 16,000 relaxation steps. At 4,000 it reported the weight 0.18 cm too low, which would have quietly shaved the headline figure. The rig-is-solved claim is what caught it, and it now demands the largest unbalanced force at any node stay under 10−8 N.

Where the credit belongs

Dietrich Braess, Über ein Paradoxon aus der Verkehrsplanung, 1968 — the road version. Robert W. Rosenthal, 1973 — the proof that congestion games have a potential function and therefore pure equilibria, which is what makes the left-hand panel solvable at all. Joel E. Cohen and Paul Horowitz, Nature, 1991 — the spring-and-string and electrical realisations, which is where the right-hand panel comes from. Empirical road-closure studies exist and are interesting; they are named here in prose rather than linked, because a citation I have not personally verified is worse on this page than no citation at all.
