The quarter you can never park in

parallel parking ↔ molecules landing on a surface

A full street of parked cars is only about three-quarters full. So is a saturated protein layer, at a different but equally fixed number. Nothing is missing and nobody is at fault — the ceiling is a property of the procedure, and you can watch it arrive.

The experiment

Two worlds, one rule. On the left, drivers arrive one at a time, aim at a random spot along the kerb, and park only if their car fits between the two nearest cars. On the right, molecules drift down onto a surface, land at a random point, and stick only if they do not overlap a molecule already bound. In both worlds nothing ever reverses out and nothing ever shuffles up to make room.

What to do: let it run and watch the two coverage percentages climb. What to watch for: the moment the red rejection outlines start to outnumber the new arrivals. That is the jam. Both numbers stop rising, a long way below 100%, and stay there no matter how many more arrivals you throw at them.

Then tick “everyone parks flush”. That changes one thing only — where a driver stops — and the ceiling on the left jumps to essentially 100%. That contrast is the whole argument.

The thesis

When things arrive one at a time, land where they happen to land, and never move again, they always stop short of full — and they stop at a number set by the procedure, not by the objects.

A street of random parkers jams at about 74.8% occupied. It does not matter whether the street is 500 car lengths or 4000; it does not matter what the cars are made of. A surface of randomly landing discs jams at about 54.7%, well below the 90.7% that the same discs reach when packed deliberately.

The missing quarter of the kerb is not a shortage of kerb. It is thousands of gaps that are each real space and each slightly too small — and it is the price of every driver choosing independently. Tick the flush-parking box and it comes back. That is the surprising part: the number is a fact about coordination, not about cars.

Going deeper

Why there is a ceiling at all

Suppose the street is nearly full. The empty space is not one long stretch; it is thousands of separate gaps left between cars that parked without reference to each other. A gap only accepts another car if it is at least one car long. Every gap shorter than that is permanently dead space, and because the arrivals were independent, the gap lengths are spread continuously from zero upward — so a fixed fraction of them are dead from the moment they form.

Nothing about this depends on how long you wait. Extra arrivals do not shrink the dead gaps; they only find the live ones faster. That is why the coverage curve flattens into a hard line rather than creeping to 100%.

The two-dimensional version is worse, and for the same reason with an extra twist: a bare patch of surface can be much larger than a molecule and still refuse it, because the patch is the wrong shape. That is why discs stop at 54.7% while cars manage 74.8%.

The named science this rests on

The process is called random sequential adsorption. Its defining features are that placements are random, irreversible, and one at a time, with overlap forbidden.

Alfréd Rényi solved the one-dimensional case in 1958, in a paper about random space filling that is usually retold as the parking problem. The limiting fraction, 0.7475979…, is now known as Rényi’s parking constant; it has a closed form as an integral but no simple expression.

Paul Flory had already solved the lattice version in 1939, from a completely different direction: he was modelling side reactions between neighbouring groups along a polymer chain, where a reacted pair strands any lone neighbour. His answer, 1 − e⁻² ≈ 86.47%, is the third check in the appendix below.

Ilona Palásti conjectured in 1960 that the d-dimensional limit is simply the one-dimensional one raised to the power d — which for discs would give 0.7476² = 0.5589. It is a beautiful guess and it is wrong; high-precision simulation has settled the disc value near 0.54707. The appendix re-derives that separation live.

Jens Feder and, independently, Yves Pomeau showed around 1980 that the approach to the jamming limit is a power law in time with exponent 1/d, not an exponential. That is what makes the last few percent unreachable in practice as well as in principle.

Random sequential adsorption is the standard model for irreversible binding of proteins and colloidal particles to a surface, which is where the second panel’s name comes from.

Is this analogy mathematical or empirical? (Mathematical — and here is exactly how far that goes)

Be clear about what this page does and does not show. Both panels run the same engine. The kerb and the surface are the same acceptance rule — propose a uniformly random position, reject on overlap, never move anything — differing only in whether positions live on a line or a plane. So the fact that both jam is not independent evidence of anything. It is a mathematical identity, and I built it in.

What is not built in, and is genuinely measured here, is where each one stops. Nothing in the code knows about 0.7476 or 0.5471; those numbers are not constants in the simulation, they are outputs. The one-dimensional answer matches a value Rényi derived analytically in 1958, and the two-dimensional answer refuses the value that Palásti’s natural-looking conjecture predicts. Both are real results about the model.

The empirical half — that real proteins landing on a real membrane actually saturate near the random sequential adsorption limit rather than near close packing — comes from the adsorption literature and is not measured on this page. Neither is the claim about real drivers: no kerb survey was involved, and real parking has bumpers, mirrors, and people who reverse. Treat the left-hand panel as the classic idealisation it is.

What would change my mind: a run in which the ceiling moved with street length, or in which flush parking also stalled near 75%, would kill the thesis outright — it would mean the shortfall came from the geometry of the objects rather than from independent irreversible choice. Both are checked in the appendix.

What failed, with the numbers

The exponent could not be measured the way I first tried

Feder’s law says coverage approaches its limit as θJ − A t−α. I tried to fit all three of those at once by grid-searching the limit and regressing the remainder log-log. It fits beautifully — r² above 0.98 every time — and it is worthless, because the limit and the exponent trade off against each other almost perfectly:

1-D, free 3-parameter fit, 3 seeds: α = 0.777, 0.692, 0.691 (predicted 1.0)
2-D, free 3-parameter fit, 4 seeds: α = 0.308, 0.235, 0.513, 0.297 (predicted 0.5)
2-D limit from the same fit: 0.5625 ± 0.0155, straddling both candidate answers

A high r² on a degenerate fit is not evidence. This is the artifact I actually found, and it is my model and my estimator at fault, not Feder.

What rescued each half, and what it cost

For the 2-D limit I stopped fitting the exponent and imposed it at Feder’s 1/2, which makes coverage linear in t−1/2 and the intercept well-conditioned. The result lands on 0.54710, and all four seeds sit below Palásti. That is a fair test of Palásti but it is no longer a test of Feder — the law is now an assumption.

For the 1-D exponent I did the opposite: I ran each seed all the way to a genuine jam, used its own measured limit, and fitted only the exponent. That gives α = 1.089, seed sd 0.172, range 0.878 to 1.420 over 8 streets — consistent with Feder’s 1, but with a spread wide enough that I would not claim better than “about 1”. The same trick is unavailable in 2-D, because a disc packing cannot be run to a true jam in finite time.

And one I nearly published with the sign backwards

My first pass measured six streets at each of three lengths and concluded that short streets pack better, by about 0.006. I wrote that down. Then the live panel above, which is only 144 car lengths, opened on a street that read 79.9% — and I went back and measured 300 seeds per length instead of six:

L = 144: 0.74435 ± 0.01672   (standard error 0.00097)
L = 250: 0.74781 ± 0.01252   (0.00072)
L = 500: 0.74661 ± 0.00841   (0.00049)
L = 1000: 0.74724 ± 0.00606   (0.00035)
L = 2000: 0.74689 ± 0.00446   (0.00026)
L = 4000: 0.74764 ± 0.00310   (0.00018)
Rényi’s exact value: 0.74760  ·  300 seeds at every length

There is no length trend at all from 250 upward — the six-seed “effect” was noise, and the one real deviation, at 144, goes the other way: 0.0033 low, about three and a half standard errors. Short streets pack slightly worse, not better. That 79.9% street was not evidence of an end effect either; over 200 seeds at that length the mean is 0.7456 and the highest single street is 0.7986. I had hard-coded the unluckiest seed in two hundred as the page’s default, which is exactly the way a cherry-picked run gets published by accident. The panel now reseeds on every load.

So read the live panel as one short street, scattering by about ±1.7 percentage points. The ceiling quoted in the thesis comes from the long streets, where that scatter is four times smaller.
