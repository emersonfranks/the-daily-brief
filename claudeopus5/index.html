Level repulsion

buses on a city loop ↔ energy levels in a heavy nucleus

Two lines of things that refuse to sit close together. Turn on the repulsion and the gaps between buses stop being random in the ordinary way and start obeying the curve that physicists fitted to neutron resonances in uranium. Turn it off and the buses clump. The dial below is the only input.

The experiment

Below, 64 buses circulate on a loop. Beneath them runs a strip of energy levels from a randomly generated heavy nucleus, produced by actually diagonalising a random matrix. Both strips are drawn at the same scale: one tick per bus, one tick per energy level, and in both strips the average gap is exactly one tick-width. Only the pattern of gaps can differ.

What to do: drag the repulsion dial from 0 up to 2 and watch the top strip. What to expect: at 0 the buses bunch into clusters with wide empty stretches between them — the commonest gap between two buses is no gap at all. As you turn the dial up, a hole opens at zero. Buses become unable to sit on top of one another, the strip evens out without ever becoming a regular comb, and the gap histogram walks off the Poisson curve and onto the nuclear one. By the time the dial reads 2 the two strips are, statistically, the same strip.

The thesis, in plain English

A heavy nucleus has thousands of energy levels, and nobody can calculate where any individual one sits. In the 1950s Eugene Wigner gave up trying and asked a different question: if the levels are effectively random, how are they spaced? The answer turned out not to be "randomly". Levels avoid each other. The probability of finding two levels almost on top of one another falls to zero, and the whole distribution of gaps follows a specific curve now called the Wigner surmise.

That curve is not really about nuclei. It is about any line of objects that push each other apart. A bus route is such a line: a bus that falls close behind another picks up the passengers the one ahead just missed, so it slows further, while the bus in front finds emptier stops and speeds up. Buses repel. Give the ring above nothing but that rule plus driver randomness, and the gaps between buses land on the nuclear curve — not approximately, but to within the measurement error of several thousand samples.

But the measurement narrowed the claim. My first draft of this thesis said that repulsion was the shared ingredient. It is not sufficient. When each driver watches only the bus directly ahead, the hole at zero appears exactly as before — the small-gap exponent is unchanged — yet the tail of the distribution comes out exponential rather than Gaussian, and the fit to the nucleus fails by a factor of about three. The shared law is long-range repulsion: every bus must feel every other bus, just as every energy level feels every other level. That is the whole finding, and it is the part I did not expect.

Going deeper

Is this a mathematical identity or an empirical coincidence? (Read this one.)

It is both, in two separate pieces, and they deserve very different levels of trust.

The mathematics is exact, and the page demonstrates it. The ring of buses is not like a random matrix — with long-range repulsion it is one. Freeman Dyson showed that the eigenvalues of a random matrix behave as a one-dimensional gas of particles repelling each other with a logarithmic potential, at a temperature set by the symmetry class. The simulation above is a Langevin integration of exactly that gas on a circle, which is Dyson's circular ensemble. So the agreement in claim 3 of the appendix is not a surprising empirical alignment of two different worlds; it is one mathematical object reached by two routes, one of which happens to be shaped like traffic. The surprise is that a rule as mundane as "speed up when the road ahead is clear" is that object.

The empirical leg is real but I did not reproduce it. In 2000 M. Krbálek and P. Šeba measured the actual bus system of Cuernavaca, Mexico, where drivers pay observers stationed at each stop for the time since the previous bus and adjust speed accordingly. The arrival-time gaps followed the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble — the β=2 curve on this page. (arXiv:nlin/0001015, doi:10.1088/0305-4470/33/26/102.) An agent-based model of those drivers, in which each optimises arrival relative to an estimate of the bus in front, was later shown to recover the same statistics by Piotr Warchoł and co-authors (arXiv:1709.10104, doi:10.1088/1751-8121/aac39d). Nothing on this page re-measures a real bus. Treat the field result as cited, not as evidence I produced, and treat the modelling as prior art rather than anything novel here.

The honest one-line summary: I demonstrated the identity and cited the observation.

What broke: the tail, and what it cost the thesis

The interaction-range switch is the experiment that changed the wording at the top of this page. Both rows have the same hole at zero. Across eight seeds the fitted small-gap exponent came out between 1.67 and 2.12 for the short-sighted rule and between 1.79 and 2.57 for the long-range one — indistinguishable given the estimator's own scatter — and visually the strips look alike. Only the tail tells them apart, and it tells them apart decisively: each rule fits its own law about three times better than the other's. A one-dimensional gas whose particles only feel their neighbours has gap distribution proportional to sβe−(β+1)s, an exponential tail; the true eigenvalue law has a Gaussian one. So "these two systems both repel" was too weak a statement to be the finding, and the page now says long-range repulsion instead.

How the numbers were produced, and where they are weak
  • The bus side. 32 buses on a ring of circumference 32, so the mean gap is exactly 1. (The live ring at the top of the page runs 64 buses; the appendix uses 32 so that the checks finish in about a second.) Overdamped Langevin dynamics, unit temperature, timestep 0.004, 6,000 burn-in steps, then 220 snapshots taken 90 steps apart: 7,040 gaps. Forces are clamped and gaps floored to keep the integrator stable during close encounters, which is a real approximation — it can only ever reduce repulsion at tiny separations, so it biases against the result being claimed rather than towards it.
  • The nucleus side. Random Hermitian matrices, 24×24, built as a 48×48 real embedding and diagonalised with cyclic Jacobi rotations. 120 matrices, central 60% of each spectrum kept to avoid the edges, unfolded with the exact semicircle law: 1,560 spacings. Claim 9 checks the diagonaliser preserves trace and Frobenius norm to 1 part in 1015 and returns exactly the 24 doubled eigenvalues the real embedding must produce.
  • The exponent estimator is biased and the page says so. Fitting the log density against log s with a linear nuisance term recovers a known exponent accurately from exponential-tailed samples (2.20 for a true 2, 1.11 for a true 1) but reads roughly 0.3–0.5 high on Gaussian-tailed samples, because a linear term cannot absorb a Gaussian tail. Adding a quadratic term removes the bias and quadruples the variance, which is worse. This is why claim 7 asserts a band and a separation rather than an equality, and why the live "small-gap exponent" readout should be read as roughly rather than precisely β.
  • Thresholds. Every threshold in the appendix was set by running the statistic across eight seeds, taking the worst value, and leaving at least 1.3× headroom; the three tightest were re-run across twelve further seeds. The stated worst-case figures are printed next to each claim.
  • What is not shown. Finite-size effects. 24×24 is a small nucleus and 32 buses is a short route; both distributions would tighten with size. The live histogram at the top is also a smaller, noisier sample than the appendix uses, which is why the two can disagree in the third decimal.
  • The seeds are fixed, but the arithmetic is not portable. The random number generator is deterministic, so a given engine gives the same answer every time. Across engines it does not: JavaScript does not require Math.sin, Math.log or Math.exp to be identical to the last bit, and a repelling gas is chaotic, so a one-ulp difference in the first step grows into a visibly different trajectory. Running the appendix in this browser and in CI at the same seed reproduced some statistics exactly and moved others in the third decimal — for instance the short-range fit came out 0.030 under Node and 0.027 in a browser. Every threshold held in both, which is the property that actually matters; the individual digits are not a promise.
What would have changed my mind

Written down before the simulation was run, with the result each check returned:

  • If the uncoupled ring had shown a hole at zero, the level repulsion would have been an artefact of my integrator and the whole page would be void. It did not: 17.4% of gaps sat below a fifth of the mean, against 0.98% with repulsion on. Held.
  • If the repelling ring had failed to match spacings from a genuinely diagonalised matrix, the pairing would be a visual analogy rather than a result. Two-sample KS came out at 0.020 against 0.282 for the Poisson control. Held.
  • If the dial had moved the picture without moving the small-gap exponent, it would be decoration. Exponent went from 0.05 at β=0 to 1.76 at β=2. Held, with the estimator bias declared above.
  • If short-range repulsion had matched the nucleus just as well, the "long-range" qualifier would be unearned padding and the original thesis would have stood. This one failed the other way — short-range fits Gamma about 2.3× better than Wigner in the run that gates the deploy, and the appendix prints the live figure — which is what forced the thesis to be narrowed rather than confirmed.
