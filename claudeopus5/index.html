Everything You Bump Into Is Bigger Than Average

The bus timetable is honest: the average gap really is ten minutes. You still wait longer than that, and not because you are unlucky. A gap twice as long has twice as much time in it for you to turn up in, so the act of arriving is itself a biased sample. The same sentence, unchanged, explains why your friends have more friends than you do: a popular person is attached to more friendships, so more people meet them. Both worlds inflate by exactly the same factor, 1 + CV², where CV is the spread of the thing being sampled.

bus arrival gaps ↔ friendship networks  ·  the shared pattern is size-biased sampling

The experiment: drag spread from left to right and watch the two big orange numbers below the panels.

They start at ×1.00 and climb together. Nothing about either world's average is changing as you drag — the buses still run every ten minutes on average and people still have six friends on average. Only the variety changes. If the two numbers stay locked to each other and to the grey prediction while you drag, that is the claim of this page, live.

Then drag sort friends by popularity up, and watch the friendship panel's number split away from the prediction while the bus panel's does not. That is the place this idea breaks, and it is the most interesting thing here.

What is actually going on

Take any list of positive numbers — wait times, friend counts, class sizes, queue lengths. Now sample from that list not by picking an entry, but by bumping into one: in a way where bigger entries are easier to hit. Every entry's chance of being drawn becomes proportional to its own size. The mean of what you draw is no longer the mean of the list. It is:

sum(x²) / sum(x)   =   mean(x) × (1 + CV²)

That is not a fit or an approximation. It is an algebraic identity, true of any finite list of numbers, and the first check in the appendix below confirms it to fourteen decimal places rather than asserting it. CV here is the coefficient of variation: the standard deviation divided by the mean. When everything is identical, CV is zero, the factor is one, and nobody is fooled. Everything else follows from that single line.

Is this analogy mathematical or empirical?

Mathematical, and that matters, because a lot of cross-domain pairings are only ever visual. These two systems are not merely reminiscent of each other; they are the same operator applied to different lists. Buses and friendships both hand you a list of positive numbers and then hand you a sampling rule that weights each entry by its own magnitude. There is no second model here that happens to fit.

What is empirical is whether each world's sampling rule really is size-biased. That is not obvious and it is what the simulation tests: dropping a uniformly random instant onto a real timetable, and asking randomly chosen people about randomly chosen friends on a real generated network. Those two things could have failed to match the identity. They do match, to within 0.50% and 1.83% respectively, and the appendix reruns both in front of you.

The two different questions you can ask a town, and why only one is exact

"Do your friends have more friends than you?" hides two different questions, and they do not have the same answer.

The first is exact for an unexpected reason: each person appears as somebody's friend exactly as many times as they have friends, so walking every friendship and reading the ends reproduces the size-biased list by construction. Rewiring the entire town cannot change it. The appendix checks this at assortativity r = 0.70 and still gets zero relative error.

The second is the version people actually mean, and it is fragile. It weights each person equally, so it depends on who the popular people are friends with. Drag the sorting control and watch it come apart.

What this page failed to reproduce

1. The everyday version of the friendship paradox is not a law

On a network wired at random, asking each person about a friend matches 1 + CV² to within 1.83% across 80 runs. Rewire that same town so popular people prefer popular friends — without changing a single person's friend count — and the answer moves at least 18.7% off the formula, and as much as 55% in the runs measured while thresholds were being set. The friend counts are identical. Only who-knows-whom changed.

This is scoped, and the scope is worth stating: the effect only appears at spread of 0.6 and above. Below that there is barely any variation in popularity, so sorting people by popularity has almost nothing to sort, and the deviation collapses to 2.7% — overlapping the unsorted noise floor. The claim in the appendix is therefore restricted to CV ≥ 0.6, and it was restricted after measuring the full range, which is exactly the kind of post-hoc narrowing that should be declared rather than hidden.

The direction of the break was not what I expected, and it is the most surprising number on the page. Sorting does not exaggerate the paradox; it very nearly abolishes it. At full spread and full sorting the live readout falls from about ×3.3 to about ×1.3. The reason is plain once seen: if popular people hoard each other, then the great majority of people — who are not popular — are left with friends as unremarkable as themselves, and almost nobody is surprised by their friends any more. The paradox is not a fact about popularity. It is a fact about popularity being randomly distributed.

2. The two panels stop agreeing at the top of the slider

Drag spread to its maximum and the bus number reaches about ×3.6 while the friendship number stalls near ×3.2 — a 12.7% disagreement between two panels that this page claims are the same phenomenon. That looked like the thesis failing. It is not: friend counts are whole numbers and nobody has fewer than one friend, so the town physically cannot realise a spread above about 1.5, while gap lengths are continuous and can. The two panels were being driven to different actual spreads by one slider.

The formula was never wrong about either world individually. The appendix tests this directly by matching the two realised spreads instead of the two slider positions; the disagreement drops to 7.1%. The live readouts show both realised spreads so the discrepancy stays visible rather than being tidied away: gaps –, friend counts –.

What would have changed my mind

Before running anything, the prediction was that both worlds inflate by 1 + CV² and that removing the spread removes the inflation. If the inflation had survived at CV near zero, the effect would have been an artefact of my sampler rather than a property of the spread, and the page would have had no thesis. That check is in the appendix as no-spread-no-illusion and it is the one designed to kill the idea. Both worlds come back to within 0.27% of ×1.00.

Where the science comes from

Neither half of this is my discovery, and the page would be dishonest if it implied otherwise.

The bus half is textbook renewal theory, where it is known as the inspection paradox or the waiting-time paradox. William Feller's An Introduction to Probability Theory and Its Applications, Volume II gives the classical treatment.

The town half is the friendship paradox, named and analysed by the sociologist Scott L. Feld in "Why Your Friends Have More Friends Than You Do", published in the American Journal of Sociology in 1991.

The random network here is the configuration model, which builds a graph from a fixed degree sequence by cutting each node into stubs and pairing them at random; it is due to Molloy and Reed, in Random Structures & Algorithms in 1995. Degree assortativity, the quantity the second slider raises and the reason the everyday version of the paradox breaks, was introduced by M. E. J. Newman in "Assortative Mixing in Networks" in Physical Review Letters in 2002.

Links are deliberately omitted: these are cited from memory, and a fabricated DOI would be far worse than none. The names, titles, journals and years are given so they can be checked. What is mine is the pairing, the simulation, and every number on this page — all of which were measured on the code you are running, not recalled from the literature.
