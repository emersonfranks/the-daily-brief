The gain knob that isn’t a telescope

a bacterium in a noisy chemical gradient ↔ an optimizer on a noisy loss surface

Both of them divide what they just sensed by how much their senses have been jittering lately. In the cell it is called variance adaptation. In the trainer it is called RMSProp. It is the same line of arithmetic, and this page runs both of them side by side to find out what it actually buys. The short answer, measured below and not what I expected: it does not help either of them steer.

The experiment, in one paragraph

Below are two panels. On the left a bacterium swims along a chemical gradient, deciding at every step whether to keep going or tumble. On the right a gradient-descent optimizer slides down a loss curve, deciding at every step how far to move. Neither can see the landscape. Each gets one noisy number per step and must act on it.

Do this. Drag measurement noise from quiet to deafening and watch the two response amplitude dials. They stay welded to 1.00 — in a cell and in an optimizer, the same number. Now switch gain control off and drag it again: both dials climb, together, at exactly the rate the noise climbs. Then look at the steering row underneath. It barely changed either time. That is the finding.

1.00

How badly each sensor reading is corrupted, in the same units as the true gradient, which is fixed at 0.40. At σ = 0.4 the signal and the noise are the same size. At σ = 16 the reading is essentially all noise. Expect steering to get worse. Watch whether the dials do.

The switch this page is about. On, each reading is divided by a running root-mean-square of recent readings before it is acted on. Off, the raw reading is used. It moves both panels at once, because the claim is that it is one switch wearing two costumes.

0.900

How much history goes into that running estimate. Slide it to 0 and the divisor becomes the current reading itself, so every response is ±1 and carries no information about how strong the signal was — steering gets measurably worse. This is the tuning cost the source paper warns about, and it is claim 9 below.

Clears both trails and both running averages and starts again from step zero with the current settings. Useful after a big change to σ, because the steering readout is cumulative over the whole run and takes a while to forget.

The thesis, in plain English

A bacterium and an optimizer both protect themselves from noise by dividing what they sense by how much their senses are shaking. It works: it pins the size of their response at 1.00 no matter how loud the world gets — measured at 0.96–0.98 across a 64-fold change in noise, in both of them, to within 0.0007 of each other.

What it does not do is help them find their way. Direction-finding falls off as roughly one-over-the-noise whichever way the switch is set: all four fitted exponents land between −0.90 and −1.04. Gain control is not a way to see through noise. It is a way to keep your response the right size while you fail to.

That second paragraph is not what this page set out to say. The prediction registered before measuring was that switching gain control off would make steering collapse. It did not. What changed was the claim, not the data; the whole story is in what failed, and two of the tests at the bottom of this page exist specifically to stop the page quietly walking it back.

What kind of analogy is this? Mathematical, not empirical. Nobody has put a bacterium and an optimizer on a bench and measured them side by side. What is shared is an update rule: v ← βv + (1−β)m², then divide by √v. That rule was derived from an optimality principle for a swimming cell and, separately, invented as a learning-rate heuristic. This page runs it in both settings and measures whether it behaves the same way. It does — but that is a statement about the equation, not evidence that evolution and Geoffrey Hinton were solving the same problem.

The deeper mechanics

What the science is, and who established it

The left panel is a one-dimensional run-and-tumble chemotaxis model. Run-and-tumble was established as the swimming strategy of Escherichia coli by Howard Berg and Douglas Brown in 1972, using the tracking microscope Berg built for the purpose. The cell cannot measure a spatial gradient across its own body — it is too small and Brownian motion rotates it too fast — so it swims, compares the concentration now with the concentration a moment ago, and uses that temporal difference to decide whether to keep going. The response law used here is the standard one: tumble probability falls exponentially with the sensed rate of change, p = p₀·exp(−λ·r), clipped to [0, 1] because a probability cannot exceed one.

The right panel is RMSProp, the adaptive learning-rate method introduced by Geoffrey Hinton and Tijmen Tieleman in a 2012 Coursera lecture and generalised by Diederik Kingma and Jimmy Ba as Adam in 2014. It keeps an exponential moving average of the squared gradient and divides each step by its square root.

The connection between them — that this division is variance adaptation, the rescaling of sensitivity by the noise currently being experienced, and that it emerges from an optimality principle in a navigating particle — is the recent finding this page is anchored to. That claim is not mine and I did not derive it; see the citations below.

What one unit means. On the bacterium side, one step is one sensing interval (biologically, of order a tenth of a second) and one unit of the horizontal axis is one body length of swimming. On the optimizer side, one step is one parameter update and one unit of the horizontal axis is one unit of the parameter being optimised. The gradient is 0.40 in both, which is what makes the noise axis comparable: σ is always measured in units of the true signal.

Why normalising cannot help you steer — the argument behind the negative result

Each step, the agent sees m = G + σξ, where G is the true gradient and ξ is a standard normal draw. All the directional information that exists in that number is contained in the ratio G/σ — the signal-to-noise ratio. Dividing m by any quantity that does not itself depend on the sign of the signal divides the signal and the noise by the same thing, so the ratio is untouched. No amount of gain control creates information that was not in the measurement.

That is why the steering exponent is near −1 in every configuration: efficiency is tracking G/σ, and G is held fixed. It is a statement about information, not about either system, which is exactly why it is the same in both.

What the division does change is everything downstream of the response, and the two systems differ there in an interesting way. The optimizer converts the response directly into a distance, so without normalisation its stride grows in proportion to the noise — a measured 30.7× over the ladder. The bacterium's stride is fixed by its flagella, so it cannot express that at all; instead the growing response slams its tumble probability against the 0 and 1 rails, on a measured 48.8% of steps at σ = 16.

What was predicted, and what failed — read this one

Three predictions were written down in research.md before any measurement, each with the outcome that would kill it. Two of them died.

registered predictionoutcome
P1 — with gain control on, steering falls off as a power law of exponent near −1. Survived. −1.039 (bacterium) and −0.897 (optimizer) for σ ≥ 1, r² 0.996 and 1.000, eight seeds.
P2 — with gain control off, steering collapses faster than any power law. Died. The fixed-gain bacterium fell off as a clean power law of exponent −0.915 with r² = 0.990. It is shallower than the adaptive case, not steeper.
P3 — gain control costs performance in quiet conditions. Died. At σ = 0.05 the adaptive bacterium scored 0.9899 against 0.9147 with gain control off. It was better, by 0.075.

Naming the artifact behind P2. The source paper reports that a non-adaptive particle's chemotactic drift collapses exponentially with noise. My model does not reproduce that, and the honest ordering of suspects puts my model first. Here is what I found. The exponential collapse comes from the mean tumble rate blowing up like exp(λ²σ²/2) when a lognormal is averaged. My model is discrete-time and acts on a tumble probability, which cannot exceed 1. Clipping it there throws away exactly the rare enormous excursions that produce the blow-up, and turns the exponential response into a threshold response — which is already noise-robust. In other words: the clip is itself a gain control, and my fixed-gain bacterium was never actually running without one. The measurement that pins this down is the 48.8% saturation figure, and it is claim 6 below.

So the correct statement of what this page tested is the small one: in a discrete-time, probability-clipped run-and-tumble model, removing variance adaptation does not degrade steering, because the probability clip performs the same normalisation. That is not a refutation of Datta and Banerjee, whose model is continuous-time and whose policy is derived rather than assumed. One page built in a day on one machine is a demonstration, not a refutation, and where it disagrees with a peer-reviewed derivation the page is the thing that is probably wrong.

What would change my mind about the surviving claim. The positive claim — that the normalised response amplitude is pinned near 1 identically in both systems — would be dead if the two systems' amplitudes diverged by more than a couple of percent anywhere on the ladder, or if the amplitude drifted with σ rather than staying flat. Both are tested below and both would show as red.

Every seed is reported. The eight seeds are fixed in the source (11, 23, 47, 91, 137, 211, 307, 419) and every published figure is their mean; none was dropped. The spread across seeds widens considerably at high noise — at σ = 16 the adaptive bacterium's individual seeds ranged from −0.018 to  0.068 around a mean of 0.019 — which is why the fits are taken over the whole ladder rather than read off any single point.

Where the sources are, and what they actually say

The recent anchor. Aniruddha Datta and Shiladitya Banerjee, Noise-robust navigation from an adaptive run-and-tumble policy, arXiv:2608.27751 [physics.bio-ph], submitted 27 August 2026. arxiv.org/abs/2608.27751 · doi:10.48550/arXiv.2608.27751. Its abstract states that variance adaptation emerges from an optimality principle, that adaptation keeps chemotactic drift finite as noise grows while a non-adaptive particle's collapses exponentially, and that adaptation carries a cost in quiet environments and requires a tuned adaptation sensitivity. That is the statement this page set out to test.

The independent supporting source. Milena D. Lazova, Tanvir Ahmed, Domenico Bellomo, Roman Stocker and Thomas S. Shimizu, Response rescaling in bacterial chemotaxis, PNAS 108(33):13870–13875, August 2011. doi:10.1073/pnas.1108608108 · free text at europepmc.org/articles/PMC3158140. Different group, different decade, different method: live-cell FRET and free-swimming microfluidics rather than theory. It reports that E. coli chemotaxis performs fold-change detection, responding to the shape of a gradient irrespective of its absolute intensity, with an adaptation timescale invariant over roughly a 10,000-fold range of background concentration. Its opening sentence is the reason this pairing exists: sensory systems rescale their response sensitivity "according to simple strategies that recur in processes as diverse as single-cell signaling, neural network responses, and whole-organism perception."

A disclosure about that second citation. The PNAS article page returns 403 to this machine and the PubMed Central mirrors are behind a bot challenge, so the record was verified through the Europe PMC REST API (PMID 21808031, PMCID PMC3158140) rather than by reading the typeset paper. The quoted sentence is the publisher's own abstract text returned by that API. This page makes no claim about that paper beyond what its abstract states.

The full research record — six candidates across six disciplines, why five were rejected against the topic ledger, and the predictions as written before measuring — is in research.md, committed alongside this page.

How this page is built

No libraries, no build step, no CDN, no network calls after the page loads. Everything is hand-rolled 2D canvas, which is the right size of tool for this job. Because nothing is fetched at runtime there is no dead-CDN failure mode to guard against.

  • policy.js — the simulation. Pure arithmetic, no DOM, seeded PRNG. This is the file node --test runs.
  • renderer.js — every pixel. Knows nothing about the physics.
  • main.js — wires controls to the simulation to the renderer.
  • claims.js — the assertions, as data, with no DOM and no test runner, so both Node and this browser import the same file.
  • policy.test.js — hands each claim to node --test.
  • claims-panel.js — renders those same claims below.
  • calibrate.js — the headless sweep the thresholds were set from.

Thresholds in claims.js were set by sweeping eight seeds at 4,000 steps, taking the worst seed, and widening by roughly a factor of two. Each one records its measured value and its headroom in a comment next to it.

Proof: run the tests here

These are not a browser reimplementation of the test suite. They are the same claims.js that node --test executes in CI, imported by this page and run on your machine. If one of them goes red here, the build does not publish.
