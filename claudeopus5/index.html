One memory, two machines

Water forcing its way through a chip full of soft channels, and magnetic domains flipping inside a lump of iron, are described by the same ninety-year-old model of two-state switches. This page runs that model and puts its strangest promise to the test: take the system on a detour and bring the dial back to exactly where it was, and every switch is back where it was too.

The experiment

Drag the drive down until tiles start flipping, then bring it back to exactly where it started. The path down and the path up are different — that is ordinary hysteresis, the thing that makes a fridge thermostat click. What is not ordinary is the destination: when the dial returns to a value it visited before, every single switch returns to the state it held there, with no record kept of the detour in between. Press Run the memory experiment to have that done precisely and counted, switch by switch.

Blue is a switch that is off: on the left a straight fibre leaving its channel open and flowing, on the right a domain pointing against the field. Amber is a switch that is on: a fibre buckled across its channel and throttling it, or a domain that has flipped to align with the field.
One tile on the left is one elastic fibre inside one microfluidic channel. One tile on the right is one magnetic domain inside the iron. The two grids look identical because they are the same switches drawn twice — that identity is the claim, and the instruments below them are what make it non-obvious, because flow rate and magnetisation are not the same quantity.

This is the only knob either machine has. Everything you see is a response to where this has been, not to where it is.

In the chip this is hydraulic cross-talk through the bypass channels; in the magnet it is the field the domains add to each other. Start at none to see the memory work, then break it with frustrated or one-way at high strength.

Cooperative coupling can be turned up until one step flips all 64 tiles at once, and the memory still holds exactly. That is the result on this page that surprised its author most.

Subharmonic response — the same cycle giving a different answer on the second pass — only showed up here in small assemblies with one-way coupling turned well up. At six switches, one-way coupling and strength 2.5, 20 of 200 random samples did it, so press New random sample and Repeat one cycle 16× a dozen times or so before concluding it never happens.

The clicks are the sound a ferromagnet actually makes: Heinrich Barkhausen heard domains flipping through an amplifier in 1919, and each click here is one switch flipping. Audio starts only when you press the button, so nothing plays uninvited.

The thesis, in plain English

A hysteron is the smallest thing that can remember: a switch with two thresholds, one to turn it on and a lower one to turn it off. Because the two thresholds differ, its state depends on where the drive has been, not where it is. Pile up a few dozen of them with scattered thresholds and you get a system with a very specific and rather beautiful kind of memory — return point memory. Close a loop in the driving and the system lands back on the exact configuration it left, every element of it, and the detour is erased.

The model was written down by Ferenc Preisach in 1935 to describe magnets. In July 2026 Abhineet Singh Rajput and Amir A. Pahlavan showed that a single elastic fibre in a microfluidic channel is a hysteron too — bistable through pure elastohydrodynamic feedback, with no moving parts, no buckled shell and no controller — and that arrays of them run from "a non-interacting Preisach regime with return point memory" into "an interacting regime with avalanche-like switching and return point memory violation". So a chip full of water is running the same equations as a lump of iron, including the specific, counter-intuitive, checkable consequence you can play with above.

Be clear about what kind of claim this is. The analogy is mathematical: one model, two substrates. It is supported empirically by the fact that each substrate has been observed obeying that model by independent groups. It is not a measurement of a real chip or a real magnet — nothing on this page was measured on hardware. The simulation above is the model itself, and the numbers below are measurements of the simulation.

Underneath

The science this rests on, and who established it

Ferenc Preisach (1935) modelled a magnet as a population of independent rectangular hysteresis loops with scattered up and down thresholds. Heinrich Barkhausen (1919) had already heard the individual switching events as crackle in an amplifier. James Sethna and colleagues (1993) showed that a random-field Ising magnet with purely positive couplings still possesses return point memory, via the no-passing property — cooperation does not destroy the memory. Frustrated or asymmetric interactions can and do destroy it, which is the boundary the controls above let you cross.

The recent work that motivated this page is listed in Sources, and the candidate shortlist it was chosen from, including the ones rejected and why, is in research.md.

How the simulation works

Each switch i has thresholds ui > di drawn at random. The drive it actually feels is the global drive plus the mean influence of the others, hi = H + c Σj Jij σj/(N−1), with σ = ±1 for the two states. Relaxation is athermal and quasistatic: while the drive rises only on-switches fire, while it falls only off-switches do, and the most-violated switch goes first. The number that fire from one increment of the drive is the avalanche size.

The flow readout is pressure multiplied by mean conductance, with an open channel at 1 and a buckled one at 0.28 in arbitrary units. That 0.28 is a model parameter chosen so the effect is visible; it is not a measured value, and none of the claims below depend on it.

The breakdown under frustration is not monotonic in coupling strength: 0 seeds failed at 0.30, 1 failed at 0.32 through 0.40, 0 again at 0.50, then 4 at 0.70 and 10 at 1.40. With 40 seeds that is too ragged to call a threshold, so this page does not report one. It reports that no failure was seen at or below 0.30 and that failures appear from 0.32 upward.

What failed, and what was concluded from it

Prediction 4 was wrong. It said avalanches of more than one switch require interaction. The first run showed avalanches of up to five switches with interaction switched off. The suspect list was ordered honestly — model first, measurement second — and it was the measurement: with 60 thresholds spread over a drive range of 2.8, a step of 0.01 is expected to sweep past 0.21 of them, so coincidences are common. Refining the step from 0.02 to 0.00025 dropped the uncoupled mean avalanche from 1.576 to 1.006 switches, while the cooperative one barely moved, from 17.0 to 12.4. The claim was rewritten to the one the evidence supports: cascades do not shrink when the step shrinks; coincidences do.

Predictions 6 and 7 were wrong. Subharmonic response — the same drive cycle giving a different state on the second pass — did not appear at all, on any of 40 seeds, at any coupling strength, with either frustrated or one-way interactions at 60 switches. Rather than quietly dropping it, the next suspect was again the model: in a 60-switch ensemble each pairwise influence is divided by 59, so no pair is strongly coupled to any other. Re-running at six switches and strength 2.5 found it: 20 of 200 samples needed two or three identical cycles to repeat themselves, and only with one-way coupling. With mutual coupling at the same settings, 0 of 200 did. That is now a claim in the appendix rather than a footnote.

What would change my mind. A single seed in which the uncoupled ensemble failed to return to its turning point would kill the central claim outright, and the appendix checks 40 of them every time you press the button. A cooperative ensemble losing its turning point would kill the second claim. If frustrated coupling never broke the memory at any strength, the page would be describing a property with no boundary, and the third claim would fail rather than being quietly dropped.
