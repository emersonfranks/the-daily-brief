The blackout and the mangrove

fireflies flashing in unison ↔ generators holding a power grid at 50 Hz

A tree full of fireflies falling into rhythm and a national grid staying synchronised are not two phenomena that resemble each other. Under the standard model of both, they are the same equation, with the same threshold, in different units. Below you are watching one population of oscillators drawn twice.

The experiment

  1. Find the tipping point. Drag coupling up slowly from zero. For a long stretch nothing happens — the flashes stay scattered and the rotor dots stay smeared around the ring. Then, over a very short range, both panels lock at once. Note the number.
  2. Move it on purpose. Now drag rhythm spread to a different value and repeat. The tipping point moves — and it lands at twice the spread, every time. The dashed marker on the coupling slider is that prediction, drawn before you get there.
  3. Knock it. Park the coupling just above the tipping point and press knock. Watch how long the trace takes to climb back — and expect it to be erratic as well as slow. Now park it far above and knock again. The same disturbance clears several times faster. A grid run close to its margin is not only nearer to failing; it is slower to shrug off everything that happens to it in the meantime.

Both panels are drawn from the same array of numbers on the same frame. Nothing is simulated twice. A dot is gold when the population has captured it and it is running at the common rhythm, and blue when it is too far off its neighbours' pace and is sliding through, never joining. How strongly each member is pulled towards the crowd. In the mangrove that is how brightly each firefly sees its neighbours through the leaves. On the grid it is how much power the transmission lines between generators can carry. Raising it is the one move that starts synchrony.

How unalike the members are when left alone. In the mangrove, the range of natural flash rates across the swarm. On the grid, how far individual machines drift from the nominal frequency with no interconnection. Widening it raises the bar for synchrony, and the dashed marker moves with it.

knock scatters every phase at random without touching the coupling — a lightning strike, or a cloud passing over the tree. It is the only way to see how fast the population pulls itself back together, which is the point of step 3.

What you are actually looking at

Put a large number of things that each tick at their own pace in a room together, and let each one feel a pull towards the average of the rest. That is the whole setup. It describes fireflies signalling through a canopy, and it describes alternators wired into the same grid, and the surprising part is not that both settle down — it is how they settle down.

Synchrony does not arrive gradually. Turn the coupling up from zero and for a long stretch you get nothing: coherence sits flat at a low value that is pure statistical noise. Then, at a threshold, order appears and climbs steeply. The threshold is not a property of the coupling alone; it is set by how unalike the population already is. Under this model it sits at exactly twice the spread of the natural rhythms, and this page measures that number rather than asserting it.

That is why the grid half matters. A grid running below its threshold is not slightly out of step, in the way a slightly overloaded road is slightly slower. It is dark. The same sharp line that decides whether a mangrove lights up in unison decides whether a country has power, and the quantity on the other side of that line is how much the machines already disagree.

What kind of claim this is. This is a mathematical analogy, not an empirical one. The assertion is that one equation — the Kuramoto model — is the standard reduced description of both systems, and that its behaviour is what you see above. It is not a claim that anyone has measured a firefly swarm and a power grid side by side and got matching numbers. Where the mapping is loose, that is spelled out below rather than left for the picture to imply.

The deeper mechanics

Collapsing the sum into a population average turns it into dθi/dt = ωi + K·r·sin(ψ − θi): nobody talks to anybody in particular, everyone talks to the average. That average is the gold arm in the right-hand panel, and its length is r.

Where the analogy is exact, and where it is stretched

Exact. Both systems are populations of phase oscillators with dispersed natural frequencies pulled towards a common phase. The threshold, the growth law, and the split between captured and drifting members follow from the equation itself, not from tuning.

Stretched, and it matters. Three simplifications sit underneath the picture above, and a reader should know all three:

  • Everyone is connected to everyone. This page uses the mean-field model, where each member feels the whole population equally. A real grid is a sparse network with specific lines, and a real mangrove is limited by what each insect can actually see. Network structure changes the threshold and can localise a failure to one region — a behaviour this page cannot show at all.
  • The grid is second-order, not first. Generators have rotational inertia, so the physically faithful description is the swing equation, which carries a second derivative. The first-order form used here is its strongly damped limit. That limit drops the inertia-driven oscillation that matters a great deal in real grid stability work.
  • Fireflies do not couple sinusoidally. They pulse. Pulse-coupled oscillators are a related but distinct model, and species differ: Pteroptyx malaccae adjusts its natural period, which plain Kuramoto does not do.

So: the shared structure is real and the threshold behaviour is a genuine property of both standard models. The animation is a faithful picture of the mean-field model, and a simplified picture of either real system.

Where this comes from

The model is Yoshiki Kuramoto's, introduced in 1975 and analysed by him through the following decade; Steven Strogatz's 2000 review in Physica D, "From Kuramoto to Crawford", is the standard account of the transition shown here. The result that the critical coupling for a Lorentzian spread is exactly twice its half-width is Kuramoto's own, and it is the prediction the dashed marker draws.

The firefly connection runs through John Buck's field and laboratory work on synchronous flashing, and Bard Ermentrout's 1991 model of Pteroptyx malaccae. The power-grid connection runs through Filatrella, Nielsen and Pedersen, who set out the Kuramoto-like reduction of a grid in 2008, and Motter, Myers, Anghel and Nishikawa's 2013 Nature Physics work on spontaneous synchrony in power-grid networks.

These are named rather than linked deliberately. A fabricated link is worse than no link, and rather than risk pointing at a plausible-looking address I have not confirmed, the authors and years are given so you can find them yourself.

What was measured, and under what conditions

Every number here came out of the same kuramoto.js that drives the animation, run headlessly. Unless stated, N = 400, explicit Euler at dt = 0.02, with natural frequencies taken as evenly spaced quantiles of a Lorentzian rather than drawn at random, which removes sampling noise that would otherwise dominate at this population size.

  • The tipping point. Scanned across four spreads (0.35, 0.5, 0.8, 1.2) and six seeds. Onset landed between 1.00 and 1.20 times the predicted 2γ. The scan advances in 4% steps, so 1.00 is the finest reading available — it cannot report low.
  • The growth law. Measured coherence against √(1 − Kc/K) at 1.2, 1.6, 2.2 and 3.0 times the threshold. Worst gap across 6 seeds × 4 spreads: 0.0084.
  • Who actually locks. The captured share matched (2/π)·arctan(Kr/γ) to within 0.0037.
  • Recovery time. Dropping the coupling to the threshold left the population needing at least 3.24× as long to fall back to incoherence as dropping it to 0.6 of the threshold. In the other direction — the one the button above tests — climbing back after a knock took at least 2.80× as long just above the threshold as far above it. Both figures are worst cases, i.e. the least impressive runs, not the best.
  • How variable that is. Recovery close to the threshold is not merely slow, it is erratic: at 1.1× the threshold, three seeds took 24s, 53s and 124s of simulated time to climb back. That spread is itself the phenomenon, and it is why the claim below tests at 1.25× rather than closer in, where a fixed step budget would time out on some seeds and not others.
What failed, what surprised me, and what would have killed the thesis

What would have falsified it. If the lock-up point had stayed put when the rhythm spread changed, the "twice the spread" law would have been a coincidence of one tuning and this pairing would be decoration. It moved every time, proportionally, across four spreads. That was the test worth failing.

A false positive I nearly published. Sweeping the coupling up and then back down left a gap of 0.09 between the two branches near the threshold. Read naively that is hysteresis, which would mean a completely different — explosive, first-order — kind of transition, and it is a far more exciting claim. It is not real. Holding the system at the threshold for progressively longer showed the descending branch decaying to the incoherent floor rather than sitting on a second stable state. Away from the threshold the two branches retrace each other to within 0.0053. The gap is critical fluctuation, made to look like memory by a measurement window that is too short exactly where the system is slowest. The claim below tests both numbers so the distinction cannot quietly rot.

A measurement I had to throw away. The first version of the test suite ran at N = 240 to stay fast in a browser tab. At that size the finite-size fluctuations were larger than three of the thresholds — the onset wandered to 1.24×, the growth-law gap hit 0.032, the branch gap hit 0.14. The tempting fix is to widen the bounds until they pass, which would have produced six green ticks that proved nothing. The population went up to 400 instead and the suite got slower.

An overclaim avoided. Below the threshold the honest answer is not zero. A finite population keeps a residue of order 1/√N — measured between 0.85 and 1.71 times that, across three population sizes and six seeds. The flat stretch at the left of the trace is small, not empty, and the last claim below exists to stop that being rounded away.
