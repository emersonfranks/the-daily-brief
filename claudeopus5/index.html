friendship networks ↔ bus waiting times

Why your friends are popular and your bus is late

Almost everyone has fewer friends than their friends do. Almost everyone waits longer for a bus than half the timetabled gap. These are not two curiosities. They are one theorem, and it says the inflation factor in both cases is exactly 1 + CV² — one plus the squared variability of the thing being counted.

This pairing is mathematical, not empirical. It is not a claim that sociology and public transport were observed behaving alike; it is that both are instances of the same identity about size-biased sampling, and the simulations below are there to check that the identity survives contact with an actual network and an actual timetable.

The experiment: drag the one slider below from left to right.

It sets how uneven things are — on the left, everyone has the same number of friends and every bus runs exactly on schedule; on the right, both are wildly uneven. Each panel draws a white marker where 1 + CV² says its coloured bar should stop. Watch the coloured bars grow to their markers and halt there, in both panels, at every slider position. The markers are not measured from the simulations; they are computed from the spread alone. The third box below is the one to keep your eye on: it divides what was measured by what was predicted, in each world separately, and both should sit at 1.00.

Unevenness redraws both worlds with the same amount of spread. Pause stops the sampler so you can read a frame. New random draw rebuilds both from a fresh seed, which is the check that you are not looking at one lucky arrangement. At CV = 0 both measured numbers should read ×1.000, and nothing surprising is left. Note that the two worlds do not end up with identical spread even though one slider set them both — a 900-person town and a 4000-bus timetable land on different realised CVs, which is why each is judged against its own prediction rather than against the other. Give the bus number a few seconds longer to settle than the friends number: long waits are rare but enormous, so its average is the slower of the two to converge.

What is actually going on

Neither system is unfair to you personally. Both are counting the wrong things.

When you sample a friendship rather than a person, someone with twenty friends is in twenty of the things you might pick and someone with two is in two. Popular people are over-represented in the sample by exactly their own popularity. When a passenger turns up at a random moment rather than picking a random gap, a forty-minute gap catches twenty times as many passengers as a two-minute one. Long gaps are over-represented in the sample by exactly their own length.

That is the same move twice: the chance of landing on an item is proportional to its size. Averaging over such a sample gives you E[X²]/E[X] rather than E[X], and that ratio is mean × (1 + CV²). Nothing in it knows whether X is a friend count or a number of minutes, which is why the same slider moves both panels together.

The consequence is worth stating plainly: the inflation is driven entirely by variance, not by the average. Doubling the buses on a route with erratic gaps helps you less than making the existing gaps even. At CV = 0 the paradox does not shrink, it disappears.

Where this comes from, and what is mine versus known

Not mine. The social half is Scott L. Feld's, published as “Why Your Friends Have More Friends Than You Do” in the American Journal of Sociology in 1991; he showed the mean number of friends-of-friends exceeds the mean number of friends whenever degrees vary at all. The transport half is older and is standard renewal theory, usually taught as the inspection paradox or the waiting-time paradox, and set out in William Feller's An Introduction to Probability Theory and Its Applications. The same identity is also taught as the class-size paradox: the average class a student sits in is bigger than the average class a registrar counts.

Mine, and only this. Building both as one simulation driven by one parameter, then checking that each lands on 1 + CV² measured against its own realised spread, and reporting where that stops being a useful way to talk about it.

What would have changed my mind. Written down before measuring: if the measured inflation had stayed flat as the slider moved, or had tracked something other than 1 + CV², or had survived at CV = 0, the thesis was dead. It is checked in the appendix below as “take the variability away and both paradoxes vanish”, and it is the test I would look at first if I were trying to knock this page down.

A prediction I made that did not reproduce

Before running anything, I predicted that the everyday phrasing — ask a random person about a random one of their friends — would give a visibly different, smaller answer than sampling a friendship directly. It did not. In the graphs built here, both estimators landed on 1 + CV² and differed only by sampling noise, within about 1.5% of each other over the seeds I ran.

The reason is a property of the generator I had not accounted for: a configuration-model graph pairs friendships at random, so a person's popularity carries no information about their friends' popularity. The realised degree assortativity of the network in the panel above is —, essentially zero. With no correlation, the two estimators coincide.

So I built the correlation in and looked again. The slider below rewires the graph so that popular people preferentially befriend popular people, and the result is sharper than my original guess: the friendship version is completely unaffected, while the everyday version collapses to nothing.

Slide it to 1.00. The first line barely moves; the second falls to about ×1.0, meaning the paradox has been abolished for anyone who learns about it by asking their friends. 


  

    The honest reading: 1 + CV² is a statement about friendship sampling,
    and the popular retelling in terms of asking people about their friends is a different quantity
    that happens to agree in uncorrelated networks. Real social networks are measurably assortative,
    so the everyday version is the one that should be quoted with care. I did not measure any real
    network here; that boundary is where this page stops.
  






  The caveat that surprised me: at high spread, the unreliable thing is the input
  

    Ask for a heavy-tailed timetable and you do not reliably get one. Requesting a coefficient of
    variation of 1.5 across five seeds produced realised values from about 1.3 to 2.6 — a single
    enormous gap can move the whole statistic. My first instinct was that the law was breaking down at
    high variability. It was not. Measured against the CV the timetable actually had, the law held to
    within 2.5%; it was only the request that was unreliable.
  

  

    That is why every number on this page is quoted against a realised spread rather than the
    slider position, and why the appendix asserts the drift explicitly rather than averaging it away.
    It is also a general warning about heavy tails: with a finite sample the sample CV is itself a
    heavy-tailed quantity, so “I set the variability to 1.5” is not a fact about the data
    you ended up with.
