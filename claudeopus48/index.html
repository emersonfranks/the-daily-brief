Why coffee and a wildfire share one magic number

Fluid soaking through porous rock and fire racing across a landscape look nothing alike. But both are asking the same question — is there a connected path from one side to the other? — and both answer it by flipping, suddenly, at the same critical density near 0.59. This is site percolation.

The experiment

Two independent random media — porous rock on the left, dry forest on the right — at the same density. Drag the slider up from dry. Below ≈0.59 each stays a patchwork of disconnected pockets. Push past it and a single cluster snaps across both at once: fluid breaks through, fire crosses. One tile = one pore, or one flammable tree.

The thesis, in plain English

Take any medium made of many little sites, each independently either "connecting" or "not" with probability p. There is a sharp critical value pc below which you only ever get small isolated clusters, and above which a single cluster suddenly spans the whole system. For a square grid with nearest-neighbour connections that number is pc ≈ 0.5927 — and it does not care whether the sites are rock pores, dry trees, cracked concrete, or friends in a rumour network. The medium is a costume; the threshold is the physics.

Where the number comes from

Site percolation was introduced by Broadbent & Hammersley (1957) to model fluid through a random porous medium — the word "percolation" is theirs, and coffee was the motivating picture. On the two-dimensional square lattice the site threshold has no closed form; it is known numerically to be pc = 0.59274621… (Newman & Ziff, 2000, and many others). The bond threshold on the same lattice is exactly ½; the site value is the one this page measures.

Finite-size scaling

On any finite grid the flip is smeared over a band of densities rather than a clean step. The width of that band shrinks like a power of the system size, so a bigger grid gives a sharper transition — exactly what the coarse→fine control shows, and what the second proof-check measures (width fell from ≈0.16 at 16×16 to ≈0.07 at 48×48 in our runs).

Is this a real analogy or a stylised one?

Two honest distinctions. First, the identical threshold across "domains" here is mathematical, and partly by construction: both panels run the same percolation engine on independent random grids, so of course they share pc. That is the point — it demonstrates the universality, it does not independently discover it. Second, real coffee and real wildfires are only approximately percolation: real grounds have correlated pore sizes and gravity; real fire has wind, moisture and ember jumps. Percolation is the idealised skeleton underneath, not a full model of either. The claim on this page is the skeleton, and only the skeleton.

What would have proved the thesis wrong

If the measured crossing had landed far from 0.5927, or if the transition had failed to sharpen with size, or if the two orientations (top→bottom vs left→right) had crossed at different densities, the "shared threshold" claim would be dead. All three were measured on the shipped code; the proof appendix re-runs them live below.
