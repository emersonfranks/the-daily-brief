What you're looking at

A silo jams for reasons that seem purely mechanical, and a crowd jams for reasons that seem purely human — impatience, fear, someone shoving at the back. So it is strange that both are controlled by the same single number, and that the number contains no psychology at all.

That number is the width of the opening divided by the width of one body. Physicists call it R = D/d. It doesn't care what the bodies are. Below roughly R = 2, both systems choke — arches form faster than they break and flow collapses to a few percent of capacity. Above roughly R = 5, both run freely and hardly jam at all. In between is the strange regime you can see above, where flow comes in bursts — a rush, a sudden lock-up, a spontaneous break, another rush.

The door in the left panel is 25 cm wider than the orifice in the right one. They behave identically anyway, because in the only units that matter, they are the same door.

The mechanism is an arch. Bodies converging on a narrow gap occasionally lock into a ring of mutual contacts spanning it, each one wedged against the next, the whole ring bracing against the sides. The load stops pressing through the hole and gets routed sideways into the walls instead. The opening is now held shut by a structure built out of the very things trying to get through it. A gap that can't fit enough bodies side by side to make an arch impossible, will eventually make one.

Which is why the second slider disappoints. Pushing harder is the intervention everybody reaches for, and a twentyfold increase in it moves throughput by a factor of about 1.4. Widening the opening from 1.2 bodies to 6 — a fivefold change — moves throughput by a factor of thirty to a hundred. Force is the variable we feel; geometry is the variable that decides. You cannot push your way through a gap that is too narrow in bodies, and you never needed to push at all through one that isn't.

The measurements, the real-world numbers, and two claims that failed

Why the flow curve has that shape

For granular discharge the classic result is the Beverloo correlation: flow scales as (D − kd) raised to a power — 2.5 in three dimensions, 1.5 in two — where k is around 1.5. The subtracted term is the important part. It says there is an empty annulus one body-width or so thick around the rim that carries no flow, so the effective opening is always narrower than the measured one. That's why the curve is so steep near the bottom and why it hits zero well before D does.

Note what's missing from Beverloo: the height of material above the outlet. A silo drains at the same rate full as nearly empty, which is why an hourglass keeps time. That's the Janssen effect — contact forces organise into chains that shunt weight into the side walls, so pressure at the base saturates with depth instead of growing. Load doesn't set flow. It's the same insensitivity you can feel on the second slider.

The clogging transition

Below R ≈ 2 an arch forms almost immediately and never breaks unaided. Above R ≈ 5, arches become geometrically implausible and flow is continuous. Between those, the system is intermittent, and the distribution of avalanche sizes between clogs is heavy-tailed — the mean burst size diverges as you approach the free-flowing end, which is the signature of a genuine phase transition rather than a gradual easing.

The same threshold has been measured outside granular matter: in sheep pushed through a gate, ants leaving a chamber, mice, and human pedestrian experiments. Same ratio, same transition, wildly different constituents. In building codes the idea survives as exit width per occupant — a rule that quietly encodes the fact that a doorway's capacity is set by body-widths, not by metres.

Two claims I tried to make and could not support

This page was originally going to argue two more famous results. Both were tested in this exact simulation before writing, and both failed to reproduce, so both were cut:

  • Faster-is-slower. The expectation, from Helbing, Farkas & Vicsek (2000), is that above some desired speed, throughput falls. In this model it doesn't. Across a twentyfold increase in drive, throughput rose — weakly and roughly monotonically — by about 1.4× in both panels. The honest statement is the weaker one now in the thesis: pushing harder helps far less than it feels like it should. The full effect appears to need conditions this setup doesn't have, and in the grain panel it shouldn't appear at all, since Beverloo predicts flow rising as the square root of driving force, which is roughly what was measured.
  • The obstacle result. Zuriguel and colleagues (2011) showed that a correctly placed obstacle above a silo outlet cuts clogging by more than an order of magnitude. Seven pillar sizes and positions were tested here. Not one beat the no-pillar baseline; the larger ones strangled the outlet completely. The effect is real in the literature but is sensitive to geometry and needs far longer runs to resolve than a live page allows.

Reporting these is the point rather than an embarrassment. Everything else on this page was kept because it measured true, on the same code you are running.

Simulation parameters

  • Social-force constants, identical in both panels: A = 2000 N, B = 0.08 m, body stiffness k = 1.2×10&sup5; kg/s², sliding friction κ = 2.4×10&sup5; kg/(m·s), relaxation τ = 0.5 s.
  • Semi-implicit Euler, four substeps per frame at dt = 1/240 s, uniform-grid neighbour search.
  • Left: 170 bodies, radius 0.25 m ±10%, driven toward the door. Right: 250 grains, radius 0.20 m ±10%, driven straight down. That drive direction is the only difference in the physics.
  • Particle colour maps accumulated normal contact force, so a red chain spanning the gap is a direct reading of the load path, not a decoration.
  • Bodies that get out are recycled to the top, holding density steady so throughput is a stationary rate rather than a decaying one.
