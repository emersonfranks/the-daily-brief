The law, in one line

In 1952 John von Neumann wrote down a result so clean it looks like a mistake. For any two-dimensional cellular structure whose walls move under their own curvature — soap film, metal grains, magnetic domains — the rate at which a cell gains or loses area depends on nothing but its number of sides:

dA/dt = k · (n − 6)

Not its area. Not how round or ragged it is. Not who its neighbours are. A hundred-sided monster and a speck both obey the same arithmetic. Six sides is the fixed point, because six is the only polygon whose 120° corners meet a soap film's equilibrium angle without any wall having to bend. Everything else is curved, and curvature is appetite.

So a five-sided bubble is not unlucky. It is doomed on arrival, and the clock started before you poured the glass.

Where it broke — and one famous law that lost

Triangles cheat. Three-sided cells shrank at 39% of the rate the law demands, and four-sided at 81%. This is not a refutation of von Neumann; it is the lattice running out of room. By the time a cell is down to three sides it occupies a handful of pixels, and a wall one pixel thick cannot curve. The law describes a continuum, and the simulation stops being one at exactly the moment a cell is about to die. Including that bin drags R2 from 0.9996 down to 0.956, which is why the headline fit is quoted over n = 5…9 and the breakdown is quoted here rather than buried.

Lewis's law lost. In 1928 Frederic Lewis claimed that a cell's area grows linearly with its side count, A(n) ∝ n − n₀ — a staple of biology textbooks for most of a century. It was fitted here against a quadratic on the same data. The quadratic won in all three seeds: R2 = 0.977 linear against 0.992 quadratic. Area rises faster than Lewis's straight line, steepening at high n. The honest summary is that Lewis's law is a decent eyeball approximation over the narrow range where most real cells live, and not a law.

Aboav–Weaire survived, emphatically. The claim that a cell's neighbours are systematically opposite to it — that big-n cells are ringed by small-n cells — predicts that n·m(n) is linear in n. Measured: R2 ≥ 0.9995 with a ≈ 1.16. Seven-sided cells really are surrounded by five-sided ones. Prosperity in this world is built directly on top of your neighbours' dying.

Why the mean is capped at six, and can only fall short

The most suspicious number on this page is the one that barely moves. Cells are born, starve, and vanish by the thousand, and ⟨n⟩ hovers at 6.000 throughout. That is not the simulation equilibrating — it is arithmetic that has no choice.

Where three walls meet at a point (and in a foam they always do — four-way junctions are unstable and snap apart within a frame), Euler's formula for a surface with no boundary forces V − E + F = 0, and three walls per vertex forces 2E = 3V. Substitute, and the average face gets exactly six edges. On a torus that is not an approximation; it is an identity.

Which is why the measured deviation is one-sided. Across 69 post-transient samples the mean read exactly 6.000 in 36% of them, sat at most 0.022 below it otherwise, and never once rose above. The identity assumes every vertex is three-fold and every cell is a simple polygon; a cell that is momentarily swallowed whole by a single neighbour has one side rather than three, and that is a debit against the total with no matching credit. The ceiling holds. Only the floor leaks.

Which sharpens the whole thing into something bleaker than it first looks. The average cell sits precisely at break-even, forever. There is no way for the population to escape into a happier configuration, because the mean is nailed down by topology. Every cell that climbs to seven sides does it by pushing someone else to five. The system is not growing. It is only redistributing, and it pays for each survivor with a death.

Where you have already seen this

The head on your beer collapses on this schedule. Small bubbles are five-sided, five-sided bubbles shrink, and their disappearance hands sides to the survivors, which is why a fine dense foam coarsens into a few fat cells before it goes flat.

The steel in a wrench does the same thing at 900 °C, only slower and in a solid. Metallurgists call it grain growth, and it is the reason annealing has to be timed: strength scales roughly as 1/√grain-size (Hall–Petch), grain size grows as √t, so leaving the metal in the furnace too long makes it measurably weaker. The micrograph of an over-annealed alloy and a photograph of stale beer foam are, statistically, the same picture.

Also on the list: mud cracks and columnar basalt, where the Giant's Causeway settles near six sides for the same reason; epithelial tissue, where the hexagon-dominated packing of cells in a fruit-fly wing follows the same census; magnetic domains in a recording medium; and the bubble rafts Bragg used in the 1940s as a physical analogue computer for dislocations in crystals — which turns out to have been a better analogy than he could have known.

Method, and what this does not show

The engine is a Q-state Potts model with a conserved cell identity per grain, seeded by a breadth-first Voronoi partition. Each sweep rebuilds the list of boundary sites and offers each one a flip to a randomly chosen neighbour's state, accepted if it does not raise the interface energy and otherwise with probability e−ΔE/kT. Time is measured in boundary sweeps, area in lattice sites, so k carries those units and is not comparable to a physical γM.

Topology is extracted from four-connected adjacency; two cells count as neighbours when they share at least three lattice sites of wall, which suppresses spurious corner contacts and is the census plotted in the histogram. The panel's ⟨n⟩ instead counts every contact, which is the quantity Euler constrains. Statistics start after 300 sweeps to clear the artificial Voronoi initial condition, during which four-fold vertices are still abundant and ⟨n⟩ runs as low as 5.84.

What this does not show: real foams drain and rupture under gravity and real metals have anisotropic boundary energies and pinning particles, none of which is modelled here. The claim being tested is narrower than "beer behaves like steel" — it is that both are curvature-driven cellular networks, and that in such a network the side count is the whole story. That part measured true to four significant figures.
